\section{Formulations Under the Naive-Attacker Standard}
\label{sec:phase4}

Every point below is evaluated under the naive/hardened external
attacker (Section~\ref{sec:threat-model-naive}), against plain Mo-PAE
and the no-privacy reference (Section~\ref{sec:residual-risk}) as the
two end-points of the achievable utility range.

\textbf{A fairness-term dominance claim can fail to survive more seeds
and more training.} An initial comparison on GeoLife, at 3 encoder
seeds and the shorter training budget used throughout
Section~\ref{sec:residual-risk}, read as dominance: ed\_soft (an
entropy-soft-weighted fairness term) achieved both higher utility and
lower decile-0 risk than plain Mo-PAE, beyond both points' confidence
intervals. We retrained both \texttt{plain} and \texttt{ed\_soft} to
convergence with 10 encoder seeds each and ran a paired bootstrap test
on the per-seed utility and per-seed decile-0 risk
(Table~\ref{tab:geolife-10seed-dominance}). At the two lower fairness
weights tested, decile-0 risk is significantly lower under ed\_soft (CI
excludes zero), but the utility difference is \emph{not} significant
(CI straddles zero) -- a real, partial privacy benefit, not dominance,
since dominance requires a significant utility gain, not merely a
utility difference indistinguishable from zero. At the highest fairness
weight tested, ed\_soft's utility is \emph{significantly lower} than
plain's, alongside the significant risk reduction -- an ordinary
trade-off, the opposite of dominance. \textbf{No formulation we test is
shown to dominate plain Mo-PAE once both training convergence and
encoder-seed count are properly controlled.} Both confounds mattered:
seed count (Table~\ref{tab:geolife-10seed-dominance} shows how much the
point estimate moves between 3 and 10 seeds) and training convergence
(the GeoLife diagnostic in Section~\ref{sec:residual-risk} found
ed\_soft's relative utility advantage over plain was itself partly an
artifact of differential convergence speed at the shorter budget).

\textbf{On Foursquare, raising ed\_soft's fairness weight moves the
operating point toward the no-privacy reference, trading privacy for
utility -- not a leveling-down pattern.} The residual-risk ratio does
rise ($3.4\times \to 15.5\times$), but the absolute risk difference
falls ($0.505 \to 0.456$) and decile 0's own risk drops in absolute
terms ($0.715 \to 0.487$) as utility rises (Section~\ref{sec:residual-risk}
reports both metrics together). The correct reading is that the
mechanism's utility and its privacy protection move together toward the
no-privacy reference point as the fairness weight rises -- the ordinary
privacy-utility trade-off, not a fairness-specific pathology. One
further complication, visible in Figure~\ref{fig:utility-risk-fronts}:
Foursquare's ed\_soft at the two higher fairness weights tested reaches
a utility that exceeds even the no-privacy reference's own ceiling once
that ceiling is trained to convergence (Table~\ref{tab:no-privacy-ceiling})
-- the same training-budget artifact diagnosed in
Section~\ref{sec:residual-risk}, not yet resolved for Foursquare's
ed\_soft specifically, since it was not retrained to convergence in
this comparison.

\textbf{A leveling-down check, recalibrated against measurement noise.}
A loss-based leveling-down check needs its harmed/helped classification
to be checked against how much apparent harm or help a seed-to-seed
comparison of the SAME mechanism produces from noise alone --
otherwise regression to the mean alone can manufacture a false
leveling-down signal. We derive harmed/helped thresholds from exactly
that null (a mechanism's own encoder-seed-to-encoder-seed variation)
and test the observed harmed rate against it before calling anything
leveling down. Across every comparison this check could be run on, none
show a harmed rate distinguishable from the null -- an earlier,
uncalibrated version of this check (a fixed epsilon threshold, no null)
had flagged several deciles as leveling down, a finding that does not
survive proper null-testing. This is most likely an underpowered test
(only 3 encoder seeds give a thin null) rather than evidence that no
redistribution is happening -- a per-decile check using GeoLife's much
richer 10-seed null is a natural extension of this work.

\textbf{Formulations are compared against a plain-Mo-PAE Pareto
\emph{curve}, not a single point.} Plain Mo-PAE's privacy weight was
swept to build a reference curve rather than relying on one operating
point (Figure~\ref{fig:utility-risk-fronts}). The curve's shape differs
sharply by dataset: GeoLife's is nearly flat -- a genuine
privacy-utility trade-off is not visible on this dataset over the range
we test, once properly converged. Foursquare's is clean and monotonic.
Gowalla-NYC's is noisier, consistent with its much smaller user count.
Because GeoLife's curve and its ed\_soft/floor points in this
comparison are at different training budgets, every curve comparison on
GeoLife in this round is unresolvable by interpolation (the
shorter-budget utility values fall below the converged curve's minimum
entirely); the Foursquare and Gowalla-NYC comparisons above should be
read as directional, for the same training-budget reason, until those
formulations are retrained to the same convergence point as the curve
itself.

\textbf{Formulations trained with a single encoder seed are shown for
visual context only.} Several additional fairness formulations
(a Lipschitz-soft penalty, a variance-of-risk regularizer, group-DRO, a
per-user minimax objective, and a group-aware adversarial-training
variant) are shown alongside plain Mo-PAE and the no-privacy reference,
but every point from this grid carries a seed-count mismatch (1 encoder
seed vs.\ plain's 3 or 10) that this paper does not resolve -- their
inclusion is for visual context, not for a dominance or leveling-down
claim, neither of which this paper makes for any of the five.
