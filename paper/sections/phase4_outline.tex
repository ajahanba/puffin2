Every point below is evaluated under the naive/hardened external
attacker (Section~\ref{sec:threat-model-naive}), against plain Mo-PAE
and the no-privacy reference (Section~\ref{sec:residual-risk}) as the
two end-points of the achievable utility range.

\textbf{The GeoLife "ed\_soft dominates plain Mo-PAE" claim does not
survive a clean, matched-epoch, 10-encoder-seed recheck.} 04d's original
claim (3 encoder seeds, 20 epochs) read as dominance: both higher
utility and lower decile-0 risk, beyond both points' confidence
intervals. Checkpoint 04e retrained both \texttt{plain} and
\texttt{ed\_soft} at 40 epochs with 10 encoder seeds each and ran a
paired bootstrap test on the per-seed utility and per-seed decile-0 risk
(Table~\ref{tab:geolife-10seed-dominance}). At $\lambda_4 \in \{0.3,
1.0\}$, decile-0 risk is significantly lower under ed\_soft (CI excludes
zero), but the utility difference is \emph{not} significant (CI
straddles zero) -- a real, partial privacy benefit, not dominance,
since dominance requires a significant utility gain, not merely a
utility difference indistinguishable from zero. At $\lambda_4 = 3.0$,
ed\_soft's utility is \emph{significantly lower} than plain's, alongside
the significant risk reduction -- an ordinary trade-off, the opposite of
dominance. \textbf{No formulation in this batch is shown to dominate
plain Mo-PAE once both epoch count and encoder-seed count are properly
controlled.} The original claim combined two confounds that both turned
out to matter: only 3 seeds (too little power, Table~\ref{tab:geolife-10seed-dominance}
shows how much the point estimate moves with 10), and only 20 epochs
(Section~\ref{sec:residual-risk}'s diagnostic found ed\_soft's relative
utility advantage over plain was itself partly an artifact of
differential convergence speed at that epoch count).

\textbf{On Foursquare, raising ed\_soft's fairness weight moves the
operating point toward the no-privacy reference, trading privacy for
utility -- not a leveling-down pattern.} The RRD ratio does rise
($3.4\times \to 15.5\times$), but the absolute risk difference falls
($0.505 \to 0.456$) and decile 0's own risk drops in absolute terms
($0.715 \to 0.487$) as utility rises (Section~\ref{sec:residual-risk}
reports both metrics together). The correct reading is that the
mechanism's utility and its privacy protection move together toward the
no-privacy reference point as $\lambda_4$ rises -- the ordinary
privacy-utility trade-off, not a fairness-specific pathology. One
further complication: Foursquare's ed\_soft at $\lambda_4 \in \{1.0,
3.0\}$ (utility $0.221$, $0.233$) exceeds even the \emph{corrected}
40-epoch no-privacy ceiling ($0.214$, Table~\ref{tab:no-privacy-ceiling}) --
the same undertraining artifact diagnosed in Section~\ref{sec:residual-risk},
not yet resolved for Foursquare's ed\_soft specifically, since it was
not part of this round's 40-epoch retraining.

\textbf{Leveling-down check, recalibrated.} \texttt{puffin/metrics/leveling\_down.py}
now uses absolute post-risk (not the per-user ratio) and derives
harmed/helped thresholds from a seed-to-seed null (the same baseline
mechanism's own encoder-seed replicates) rather than a fixed epsilon,
testing the observed harmed rate against that null before calling
anything leveling down. Rerun on 04d's existing comparisons: \textbf{0 of
40 computed comparisons} show a harmed rate distinguishable from the
null -- the earlier, uncalibrated version's findings (GeoLife floor:
5--6 of 10 deciles; Gowalla-NYC ed\_soft $\lambda_4=0.3$: 10 of 10
deciles) do not survive proper null-testing. This is most likely an
underpowered test (only 3 encoder seeds give a thin null) rather than
evidence that no redistribution is happening -- a per-decile check using
GeoLife's much richer 10-seed null (90 ordered pairs vs. 6) is a natural
follow-up, not yet run.

\textbf{Formulations are now compared against a plain-Mo-PAE Pareto
\emph{curve}, not a single point.} Per the PI's instruction, plain
Mo-PAE's privacy weight ($\lambda_3$) was swept (40 epochs, 3 encoder
seeds) to build a reference curve rather than relying on one operating
point. The curve's shape differs sharply by dataset: GeoLife's is
nearly flat (utility $0.722$--$0.758$, decile-0 risk $0.337$--$0.348$
across the whole sweep) -- a genuine privacy-utility trade-off is not
visible on this dataset over this range, once properly converged.
Foursquare's is clean and monotonic (utility $0.051$--$0.210$ as
$\lambda_3$ falls). Gowalla-NYC's is noisier, consistent with its much
smaller user count. Because GeoLife's $\lambda_3$ curve and 04d's
ed\_soft/floor points are at different epoch counts, every curve
comparison on GeoLife is currently unresolvable (04d's utility values
fall below the 40-epoch curve's minimum entirely); Foursquare and
Gowalla-NYC comparisons are reported in
\texttt{reports/04e\_reference\_curve.md} as directional only, for the
same epoch-count reason.

\textbf{The five single-encoder-seed formulations (lipschitz,
variance, group-DRO, per-user minimax, group-adversarial) are not
retrained this round.} They are shown alongside plain Mo-PAE and the
no-privacy reference for the first time (Checkpoint 04c could not plot
plain Mo-PAE's utility at all), but every point from this grid carries
a seed-count mismatch (1 encoder seed vs.\ plain's 3) that this paper
does not resolve -- their inclusion here is for visual context, not
for a dominance or leveling-down claim, neither of which this paper
makes for any of the five.
